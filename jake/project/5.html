Retirement Savings Options

  • Traditional IRA (Individual Retirement Account)
    • Tax Treatment: Contributions may be tax-deductible depending on your income and whether you or your spouse are covered by a retirement plan at work. Earnings grow tax-deferred.
    • Withdrawals: Distributions are taxed as ordinary income. Withdrawals made before age 59½ are typically subject to a 10% early withdrawal penalty, although there are exceptions (e.g., first-time home purchase, qualified education expenses, disability).
    • Contribution Limits: The IRS sets annual contribution limits. For 2023, the limit is $6,500 for individuals under 50 and $7,500 for those 50 and older (this includes a $1,000 catch-up contribution).
    • Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs): RMDs must start by April 1 of the year following the year you turn 73. Failure to take RMDs can result in a hefty penalty.
  • Roth IRA
    • Tax Treatment: Contributions are made with after-tax dollars, meaning you don't get a tax deduction for contributions. However, qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
    • Withdrawals: Contributions can be withdrawn at any time without penalty or taxes. Earnings can be withdrawn tax-free if the account has been open for at least five years and the withdrawal is made after age 59½, or due to disability, death, or a first-time home purchase (up to $10,000).
    • Contribution Limits: Same as Traditional IRA ($6,500 for individuals under 50 and $7,500 for those 50 and older in 2023). However, there are income limits for contributing to a Roth IRA. For 2023, the ability to contribute phases out for single filers with modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) between $138,000 and $153,000, and for married couples filing jointly with MAGI between $218,000 and $228,000.
    • No RMDs: Unlike Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs do not require withdrawals during the owner's lifetime.
  • 401(k) Plans
    • Tax Treatment: Contributions are typically made with pre-tax dollars, reducing your taxable income for the year. Earnings grow tax-deferred.
    • Withdrawals: Distributions are taxed as ordinary income. Early withdrawals before age 59½ are usually subject to a 10% penalty, with some exceptions (e.g., hardship withdrawals, rule of 55, substantially equal periodic payments).
    • Contribution Limits: For 2023, the contribution limit is $22,500 for individuals under 50, and $30,000 for those 50 and older (including a $7,500 catch-up contribution).
    • Employer Match: Many employers offer matching contributions, which is essentially free money towards your retirement.
    • RMDs: Required minimum distributions must begin by April 1 of the year after you turn 73, unless you are still working and not a 5% owner of the business sponsoring the plan.
  • Annuities
    • Types: There are several types of annuities including fixed, variable, and indexed annuities. They can be immediate (payments start almost immediately) or deferred (payments start at a future date).
    • Tax Treatment: Contributions are made with after-tax dollars, and earnings grow tax-deferred. Upon withdrawal, earnings are taxed as ordinary income.
    • Withdrawals: Withdrawals before age 59½ are typically subject to a 10% penalty. Additionally, annuities often have surrender charges if you withdraw funds within a certain period after purchase.
    • Payments: Annuities provide a stream of income that can be guaranteed for a specific period or for life, offering protection against the risk of outliving your savings.
    • No Contribution Limits: Unlike IRAs and 401(k)s, there are no annual contribution limits for annuities, making them a potential option for high-income individuals seeking additional tax-deferred growth.
